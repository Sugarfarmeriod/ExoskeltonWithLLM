## method_relevant_8
1. Existing methods for intention recognition so far can only be used for several tasks that are predefined in advance, thus none of them allow patients to control the hand exoskeleton completely at will. To surpass this limitation, an LLM-enabled incremental learning framework that integrates a hand exoskeleton controller with Large Language Model (LLM) is proposed and validated in this study. The framework offers patients an intuitive interface via voice interaction and enables patients to perform not only predefined operation tasks by the hand exoskeleton controller but also non-predefined ones that can be learned from the LLM. As a result, the hand exoskeleton controller continues to learn from the LLM, therefore is gradually able to perform all tasks in daily life. This pioneering study paves a new way in building patient-controlled hand exoskeletons with autonomous intelligence that can deal with non-predefined operation tasks in unstructured environments.
2. Index Terms— Hand exoskeleton, motion intention recognition, large language model (LLM), ChatGPT, incremental learning. I. I NTRODUCTION T HE global prevalence of stroke has reached epidemic proportions with more than 12.2 million new cases reported annually and over 110 million survivors in the world [1]. Strokes often disrupt neural connections between the brain and muscles of patients, leading to muscle atrophy, and further hand weakness or paralysis [2]. Given that a hand accounts for approximately 90% of upper limb functionality [3], [4], its impairment significantly deteriorates the life quality of stroke survivors. Recent years have witnessed an upsurge in the development of hand exoskeletons for post-stroke rehabilitation [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. However, few have been specifically designed to assist patients in their activities of daily living (ADLs).
3. It is noteworthy that a large proportion of stroke survivors are left with a hemiparetic hand after the post-stroke six-month golden recovery period [10], [11]. Therefore, there is a great demand for assistive hand exoskeletons designed for ADLs. Despite their increasing popularity, they are often not reliable in detecting hand movements, which is attributable to the inherently low signal-to-noise ratio of EEG signals and the weakness of EMG signals originating from the hemiparesis. As a result, they are rarely used for assisting patients in ADLs. Body movements are usually very difficult to generate a complex motion intention. Our recent study uses a foot-machine interface to control a hand/forearm exoskeleton for restoring voluntary bimanual activities of stroke survivors [26]. However, such foot-machine interface needs to map foot movements to hand gestures, thus is not intuitive and requires an intensive training on patients before it can be used. In contrast, voice control offers an appealing alternative for patients to control the hand exoskeleton, which is not only robust and simple but also remarkably intuitive and natural.
5. By incorporating the LLM with the hand exoskeleton controller, it is equivalent to implanting a brain into the hand exoskeleton controller, and thus the hand exoskeleton controller may gain from the LLM the capability of executing unpredefined ADLs by the hand exoskeleton. Several studies have shown the potential of LLM used for robot motion planning via dialogues between users and the LLM [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]. These LLM-controlled robots directly use the LLM as their controller to execute some rudimentary tasks under predefined scenarios, such as picking and placing blocks. However, our preliminary findings revealed that using LLM as the direct hand exoskeleton controller to perform hand motions fell short in terms of response speed. It takes a relatively long time for the LLM to parse voice commands into hand motion commands, thus it is not suitable for ADLs which often require swift responses.
6. Moreover, commercially available LLMs are expensive [34] and they may not be affordable for patients in their everyday life. Consequently, some researchers dedicated to developing open-source LLMs that can be deployed locally [35], [36], [37]. Unfortunately, such local LLM demands a high-performance hardware platform to run efficiently, which will inevitably increase the cost of the hand exoskeleton. To the best of our knowledge, there still lacks an effective and low-cost method that utilizes LLM in hand exoskeleton control to offer superior performance compared to conventional strategies. This paper reports a study on developing an LLM-enabled incremental learning framework for hand exoskeleton control by integrating a hand exoskeleton controller with an LLM, named ChatGPT [38]. The incremental learning framework allows the hand exoskeleton controller to gradually learn from the LLM when encountering non-predefined tasks and subsequently memorize these new tasks for future operations

8. Hand Exoskeleton Control The schematic diagram of the LLM-enabled incremental learning framework for hand exoskeleton control is shown in Fig. 2. A user controls the hand exoskeleton through a speech recognition module, which converts the user’s vocal instructions into text commands; Then, a NLP translates the text commands into finger motion commands; These finger motion commands are subsequently fed into a finger motion controller to control the hand exoskeleton to perform corresponding hand motions. The user judges whether the generated motions are appropriate to complete the task. If it is not, the LLM-enable incremental learning module is called to generate new commands and the controller expands the command set by dialoguing with the customized LLM. As a result, the hand exoskeleton controller incrementally learns new tasks to enable the user to perform more and more ADLs with the hand exoskeleton.

D. Discussion It has been a formidable challenge to recognize patient’s motion intention to control the hand exoskeleton devices for a long time because human hand motions are extremely complicated. Existing control methods can only enable patients to perform several predefined hand gestures, and impossible to control the hand exoskeleton completely at will. In contrast, we develop a voice control system for hand exoskeletons, offering patients an intuitive interface as easy as talking to the voice assistant in a smart phone. Moreover, the proposed incremental learning method empowers patients to progressively expand controllable hand motions during daily use of the hand exoskeleton. As a result, patients can eventually achieve free control over their paralyzed hands to execute ADLs.

C ONCLUSION This paper presents a pioneering study of incorporating LLMs in the field of hand exoskeletons, which will revolutionize the way to control hand exoskeletons by patients. The proposed LLM-enabled incremental learning framework enables patients to control the hand exoskeleton as easily and conveniently as a voice assistant in a smart phone. It embeds LLMs as expert modules that provide guidance according to the human experience when encountering tasks that are unknown. By this way, the hand exoskeleton controller can incrementally learn to complete ADLs during routine use, eventually eliminating the requirement of consultation from the expert modules.
