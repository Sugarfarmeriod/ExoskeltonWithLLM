## Abstract
Exoskeletons can assist humans during squatting and the assistance has the potential to reduce the physical demands. Although several squat assistance methods are available, the effect of personalized assistance on physical effort has not been examined. We hypothesize that personalized assistance will reduce the physical effort of squatting. We developed a human-in-the-loop Bayesian optimization scheme to minimize the metabolic cost of squatting using a unilateral ankle exoskeleton. The optimization identified subject-specific assistance parameters for ascending and descending during squatting and took 15.8 min on average to converge. The subject-specific optimized condition reduced metabolic cost by 19.9% and rectus femoris muscle activity by 28.7% compared to the condition without the exoskeleton with a higher probability of improvement compared to a generic condition. In an additional study with two participants, the personalized condition presented higher metabolic cost reduction than the generic condition. These reductions illustrate the importance of personalized ankle assistance using an exoskeleton for squatting, a physically intensive activity, and suggest that such a method can be applied to minimize the physical effort of squatting. Future work can investigate the effect of personalized squat assistance on fatigue and the potential risk of injury.

Robotic Ankle-Foot Orthosis Emulator We used a tethered ankle-foot orthosis emulator for squatting assistance. The emulator comprises an ankle-foot orthosis end-effector ( Figure 2A), two actuators (Humotech, Pittsburgh, USA), and an off-board controller (Speedgoat Inc., Massachusetts, USA) ( Figure 1A). The exoskeleton provided active assistance using tethered motor actuation for plantarﬂexion and passive assistance through elastic bands for dorsiﬂexion.
3. The range of motion of the device assistance was 140 degrees, which is larger than the humans’ ankle angle range [56], and the worn weight was 1.4 kg. Considering the importance of the weight and range of motion [27], [28], this device is an ideal platform for providing squat assistance with a high torque capability: a maximum of 140 Nm. Subjects wore the robotic ankle exoskeleton end-effector on their dominant lower limb using two Velcro straps, one on the shin area and the other on the metatarsal. An additional Velcro strap was placed around the heel to restrict lateral movement of the foot. B. Controller Design The controller provided the torque used to assist the user during a squat based on the ankle angle using ascending and descending parameters associated with the squat phase. The ascending and descending parameters were free control parameters that were optimized for each subject. The squat phase was determined by a heuristics-based squatting state estimation method.

Bayesian Optimization Bayesian optimization (BO) is a non-parametric, sampleefﬁcient, and noise-tolerant close-to global optimization method that is well suited for applications where the cost function is expensive to evaluate. HIL BO typically uses a physiological signal from a subject to determine the cost of certain assistance given a parameter; hence the optimization includes the human in the optimization loop [12], [16], [51], [52]. In this study, HIL BO optimized a two-parameter set, corresponding to the parameters for the ascending and descending state of the squatting controller. First, we initialized the BO by exploring four randomly selected sets of parameters.
6. During the exploration, assistance was provided to a subject using the parameters while the metabolic cost of squatting was measured. After collecting the initial exploration data, the optimization estimated the posterior distribution of the metabolic cost of squatting using an exoskeleton parameter set via Gaussian process [57], [58]. Then, the acquisition function, Expectation Improvement, used this posterior distribution information to select a new pa rameter set by maximizing the log-likelihood [59], [60]. The parameter was used to determine the assistance provided to a subject, and the subject squatted with the assistance while the respiratory rate was measured to indicate the metabolic cost for the condition. This HIL BO was iterated until either 18 minutes had passed or the optimization selected th e same parameters three times consecutively, as this was considered to indicate convergence. Metabolic Estimation With Early Stopping Process We used the metabolic cost of squatting as a cost function in the Bayesian optimization. We estimated the metabolic cost using the phase-plane based metabolic estimation [61] with the estimation stopping process. The phase-plane based method converts metabolic cost in the time domain to the phase domain by having the metabolic rate on the horizontal axis (x-axis) and the forward difference metabolic rate on the vertical axis (y-axis) ( Figure 1B). In this phase domain, the steady-state metabolic cost is the point on the metabolic rate trajectory that meets the x-axis. The remaining section of the trajectory is deﬁned as the transient phase. The transient trajectory was used to determine the s teady-state metabolic cost by conducting real-time regression using the Variational Bayesian Gaussian Mixture Model (VBGMM) [62].

3. D ISCUSSION We investigated the effect of personalized assistance provided by a robotic ankle-foot orthosis on the physical effort needed to perform a squatting activity. We hypothesized that the squat effort would be reduced when optimized assistance was provided by the robotic ankle-foot orthosis. We developed a squat assistance method using a unilateral robotic ankle-foot orthosis during the ascending and descending phases of the squat. Each ascending and descending phase had a free control parameter that was optimized using HIL optimization to minimize the metabolic cost of squatting for each subject. The rapid HIL optimization with the faster metabolic cost estimation was able to identif y subject-speciﬁc assistance within 16 minutes. The assistance parameters were widely varied depending on the subject ( Figure 4D). Subject-speciﬁc assistance reduced the metabolic cost of squatting by 19.9% and RF muscle activity by 28.7% compared to the condition without th e device.
4. To reduce such muscle activities, squat assistance has typically been provided using a knee exoskeleton [78], [79], and little attention has been devoted to ankle assistance. Ankle assistance can inﬂuence not only ankle muscle activities [80]–[82] but also the activities of other muscles, such as knee ﬂexion muscles during running [81] and walking, especially during the double-support phase [82]. Similar to the double-support phase, the squatting posture forms a closed kinematic chain; hence, ankle assistance can inﬂu ence the quadriceps muscles. The directly assisted ankle muscles showed a tendency of reduction when personalized assistance was given. Future work can include a muscle synergy analysis to fully understand muscle recruitment and coordination patterns [80]. The optimized assistance was w idely varied across participants, which suggests the importance of personalization.
5. The biomechanics of squatting can be varied due to subject-speciﬁc biomechanics [4] and natural range of motion [49], [69]. These subject-speciﬁc characteristics appear to contribute to a unique metabolic rate landscape for each user ( Figure 5), resulting in personalized assistance parameters for both ascending and descending conditions ( Figure 4D) and unique torque proﬁle (Figure 4E). The rapid HIL optimization with the faster metabolic cost estimation was able to identif y subject-speciﬁc assistance within 16 minutes. This fast convergence in HIL optimization enabled optimized assistance for a strenuous squatting activity. This result suggests that this optimization method could be applied to other intensive activities or to individuals with limited strength who have a more limited experimental time budget. Also, the method can help further avoid participant fatigue due to a prolonged trial [83].
