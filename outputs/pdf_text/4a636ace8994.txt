These approaches typically rely on state-machine-based control with impedance controllers tailored to different locomotion phases, ensuring appropriate assistance across various activities and environments. However, these methods necessitate lengthy calibration procedures, as many impedance parameters need to be fine-tuned to provide appropriate assistance for various activities (e.g., overground walking, ramps, and stairs). Methods This study presents three contributions: (1) a state-machine-based control strategy for partial assistance lower-limb exoskeletons, (2) a computational method to extract reference trajectories from a benchmark dataset (Camargo et al. in J Biomech 119:110320, 2021), enabling the identification of state-machine controller parameters and simplifying calibration procedures and (3) a dataset of 19 healthy individuals walking in five walking conditions (overground walking, upstairs, downstairs, up ramps, and down ramps) using either the state-machine approach or a transparent controller. Results The state-machine controller produced in average more negative interaction power (−2.6 × 10−2 W/ kg) compared to transparent control (0.8 × 10−2 W/kg), indicating greater user assistance. Preferred walking speed was notably faster with the state-machine controller, particularly on level ground, ramps and stairs ascent (25–32% increase). Kinematic analysis revealed closer alignment to able-bodied gait patterns with the state-machine controller, suggesting improved gait quality. At the same time, the dataset of the collected locomotion activities (dataset link) will constitute a new benchmark dataset for locomotion. Conclusions In this work, we presented and evaluated a novel state-machine-based control strategy for partial- assistance lower-limb exoskeletons. Journal of NeuroEngineering and Rehabilitation (2026) 23:114 Introduction Lower-limb exoskeletons are used to support physi - cal rehabilitation in clinical settings [ 2, 3]. In particular, weight-bearing exoskeletons, which interface with the lower extremities, include rigid robotic components that enable multi-joint support and direct transfer of loads to the ground [4]. Assistive exoskeletons are generally posi - tion-controlled [5], which limits the amount of voluntary motion from the patient. Conversely, partial-assistance exoskeleton controllers are ideally suited for patients with mild to moderate impairments, as they assist in movement while encouraging the patient’s active engage- ment [4, 5]. Different approaches have been implemented for rehabilitation settings, from assistance (e.g., assist-as- needed) to resistance (e.g., error augmentation), demon - strating their ability to enhance motor learning [6, 7]. Generally, partial-assistance controllers are designed using a state-machine approach [ 4], where the control - ler identifies both the ongoing activity and its current state, assigning a corresponding robotic behavior to each phase. This method is typically structured hierarchically into high-level, mid-level, and low-level control layers. The high-level controller estimates the desired behavior tailored to specific ambulatory activities, such as walk - ing on flat terrain, stairs, or ramps [ 8, 9]. The role of the mid-level controller is to estimate the various states within an activity, for example, identifying the gait states (e.g., swing and stance) during walking and setting a desired interaction torque behavior accordingly [ 10]. The interaction torque behavior is typically modeled by an impedance controller using virtual springs and damp - ers, characterized by stiffness and damping parameters, which are attached to reference joint positions. Finally, the low-level controller compensates for the exoskeleton’s dynamics and generates motor commands, based on the desired interaction torque profile identified by the mid- level controller [11]. The number of gait states defined in this state-machine approach increases with the number of activities encoded in the high-level controller, as well as the complexity of the middle-level controller. In other words, each torque behavior requires prior identification of control param - eters tailored to the specific activity being performed and the corresponding phase of locomotion. The result - ing complexity of these hierarchical approaches impacts the time required for calibrating assistive device con - trollers. This problem has been extensively observed for active prosthetic devices [ 12], while only a few previous works specify the amount of time spent on the controller calibration for lower-limb exoskeletons [13]. This is likely due to a reliance on pre-set trajectories and a lack of adaptability, which reduces the need to customize param- eters for individual users. Furthermore, most research has focused on overground walking, with limited explo - ration of scenarios involving stairs and ramps [4]. In prosthetics, various approaches have been proposed to derive an optimal set of impedance parameters from benchmark datasets of healthy individuals, which can then be utilized for controlling wearable robots [ 14– 16]. These datasets have been employed to identify normative locomotion characteristics [ 17, 18]. However, normative datasets [1, 19– 21] of healthy individuals engaging in various locomotion activities exhibit substantial differ - ences when compared to locomotion performed while wearing an exoskeleton, particularly weight-bearing exoskeletons. This gap is primarily due to common exo - skeleton design characteristics, particularly their rigid - ity and actuation only in the sagittal plane, which have been shown to alter users’ movement execution [22]. This alteration requires users to adopt compensatory strate - gies that can compromise movement quality compared to natural walking. The diverse objectives in exoskeleton research, even within partial assistance, may explain the lack of works akin to prosthetics. Varying patient abilities pose unique control challenges and often require longer calibration to account for residual function in restoring normative movement. For these reasons, our contribution is threefold: (1) First, we propose a state-machine-based control strategy for a lower-limb exoskeleton aiming to assist movement in different locomotion modes. (2) Second, we estimate a set of reference trajectories from a benchmark data - set [ 1] of able-bodied gait across various terrains: over - ground, ramps (ascent and descent), and stairs (ascent and descent). (3) Finally, we present a normative data - set of 19 healthy individuals wearing a lower-limb exo - skeleton controlled both with a state-machine approach (SM) and a transparent (TR) controller designed to minimize interaction forces. The two controllers have been validated and compared to walking without an exo - skeleton (NE) under five locomotion conditions: over - ground walking (W), stairs ascending (SA)/descending (SD), and ramps ascending (RA)/descending (RD). This dataset can be used in the future to fine-tune the state- machine approach and for machine-learning-based con - trollers [23]. In fact, this dataset contains a rich set of information, including kinematic features and interac - tion torque data for both the state-machine approach and controllers. The proposed controller will be applied to patient populations, while the dataset will serve as a valuable resource for advancing robust and effective control mechanisms through machine learning techniques. Exoskeleton whole-body controller In this section, we present the general structure of the exoskeleton utilized in this work, together with the low-level controller designed to compensate for the dynamics of the exoskeleton, which is described in detail in [11]. A six-degrees-of-freedom lower-limb exoskeleton (ExoMotus-X2, Fourier Intelligence, Shanghai, China), shown in Fig. 1A, was adapted and used as a platform to test the proposed state-machine controller. This exoskel - eton has four active degrees of freedom at the hip and knee joints, and passively allows two degrees of freedom at the ankle joints. The active joints provide peak torque of 80 Nm, maximum power of 100 W, and a joint speed limit of 3 rad/s. Joint torques are computed using four strain gauges on the link distal to each active joint. An inertial measurement unit (IMU) (Tech-IMU V4, Tech - naid S.L., Spain) is mounted on the backpack to measure the orientation and angular velocity of that link. Force- sensitive resistor-based footplates were installed beneath the soles of the exoskeleton to measure the forces between the user-exoskeleton couple and the ground. Each footplate is equipped with 16 force-sensitive resis - tors, amounting to a total of 32 sensors across both foot - plates. These sensors interface with the ground through a rigid aluminum sole and rubber bearings, adding a degree of compliance. The controller estimates the interaction torque between the user and exoskeleton ( τ int) indirectly through strain gauges and defines a desired interaction torque ( τ ∗ int). The transparent condition is used in this work as a base - line for comparison to the state-machine approach when no explicit assistance is provided to the user. Details of the WECC controller, along with compre - hensive analyses of its transparency and rendering per - formance, are provided in [11]. State-machine controller In this section, we present the novel State-Machine (SM) controller that assists movement during locomotion activities as well as the process of extracting parameters for the controller across the different conditions. The SM controller is implemented by defining an interaction torque profile (τ ∗ int ̸=0 ) modeled as a spring-damper sys - tem converging toward specific equilibrium angles and joint velocities specific for each locomotion activity (W , SA, SD, RA, RD) and gait phase. As a high-level con - troller, the system transitions between the five activities based on a manually triggered remote connected to the exoskeleton. As a middle-level controller, each activity was seg - mented into four gait phases for each leg: Early Stance (E-St), Late Stance (L-St), Early Swing (E-Sw), and Late Swing (L-Sw). The choice of four phases, instead of fewer or more, is driven by our goal of preserving movement complexity while minimizing the number of parameters that need to be adjusted in real-time to accommodate each user’s needs. The transition between gait phases is displayed in Fig. 2 and determined by two threshold values. The first threshold, ( θhip,T ) controls the tran - sition from early stance (or swing) to late stance (or swing) and is compared to the actual hip angle of the left (θhip,l) or right leg ( θhip,r ). This threshold is set pro - portionally to the equilibrium angles of the SM, defined as θhip,T = θeq hip − ϵ. The second threshold, αT , on the other side, governs the transition between the stance and swing phases. It dictates when the system switches from stance to swing and vice versa, ensuring a smooth pro - gression through the gait cycle. The choice of αT signifi- cantly affects the controller’s reactivity during heel strike and toe-off. Specifically, a smaller threshold results in a more reactive controller but can lead to instability in the presence of noise. Based on a preliminary evaluation, we selected αT = 0.2 for all experiments. Transitions across the different states are synchronized between the two legs, and therefore only six different combinations of left and right states are possible. Experimental setup In this section, we present the user population and the experimental protocol with particular attention to the different locomotion performed and collected in the dataset attached to this work. Table 2 presents the demographics of the 19 (11 males, 8 females) healthy subjects (age 29.6 ± 6.8, height 1.75 ± 0.09 m, weight 69.5 kg ± 11.7 kg) who participated in the study. One participant declared having already walked with an exoskeleton (not ramps or stairs), and others declared no previous experience. The institutional review board of Northwestern University (STU00212684) and the Ethical Board of the Hospital Universitario Severo Ochoa, (Madrid, Spain) approved this study, and all procedures were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was performed in parallel at two sites with similar infrastructure: The experimental setup in Chi - cago (Fig. 1B) comprised 6 m of overground walking, a staircase with six stairs of 150 mm height, and a 5° ramp of 4 m in length. The experimental setup in Madrid (Fig. 1C) comprised of 13 m of overground walking, a staircase with six stairs of 170 mm height, and a 6° ramp of 3 m in length. Experimental protocol At both sites, participants began the experiment per - forming all the locomotion activities without wearing the exoskeleton (NE condition). Their motion was recorded using a camera system and used to estimate their loco - motion characteristics. After this recording, the exoskel - eton was fit and worn by each participant. At the start of the controller, the ramp slope and stair height were man- ually specified. Participants always started the session performing overground walking, followed by stairs and ramps in a randomized order. Overground walking was always the first activity to allow participants to familiar - ize themselves with the exoskeleton for safety, adapting to the constraints imposed by the exoskeleton (i.e., actua- tion only in the sagittal plane). The Transparent (TR) and State-Machine (SM) conditions were tested during each locomotion activity of the experiment; the order of TR and SM was randomized. Before each condition and activity, a familiarization phase was provided to each participant. During this phase, the stiffness and damping parameters of the SM were manually adjusted according to each user’s preference to achieve comfortable walk - ing, taking into account the user’s weight. This drastically reduces the number of parameters to tune online (stan - dard SM has 16 stiffness and damping parameters and 16 equilibrium positions and velocities; here only 8 were potentially adjusted). At the same time, the familiarization time varied across participants: typically, two iterations for each condition and activity were sufficient to adjust one’s strategy to the exoskeleton. After the familiarization phase, participants were instructed to walk at their self-selected speed, for each condition and activity. For each condition (TR & SM), participants performed 5 iterations of Walking (W), 3 iterations of Stairs (SA/SD) and 3 iterations of Ramps (RA/RD). Data analysis In this section, we describe the metrics used in the analy - sis of the dataset collected. Interaction power The first metric presented in this study is the interac - tion power, normalized by each participant’s weight, as defined in [11]. Interaction power was computed for each joint (hip and knee), participant j and during each stride i, using the equation pi,j = ∑T i t=0 ˙θ(t)τint(t) Tw j , (10) where ˙θ(t),τint(t),w j are respectively the joint velocity at time t, the interaction torque at time t and the weight of the user. By choice of reference frame, negative power represents assistive torque provided by the exoskeleton to the user (i.e. interaction torque direction equal to movement direction), whereas positive interaction power indicates resistance. The interaction power for the knee was obtained by summing the left and right knee power values; the same applies to the hip interaction power. The primary hypothesis is that the SM condition of the exoskeleton will provide greater assistance compared to TR during all locomotion activities, indicated by more negative power. To analyze this particular metric, we employed a linear mixed-effects model to investigate the impact of various factors on interaction power summed over knee and hip. The linear mixed-effects model, fit - ted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation, accounted for fixed effects of locomotion mode (W, SA, SD, RA, RD), joint (hip/knee), conditions (SM/TR), and their interactions. To capture individual variations, we incorporated random intercepts for each user. Follow - ing Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, the significance threshold for comparing interaction power across locomotion modes and joints was adjusted to 0.005. Kinematic analysis Kinematic analysis was performed in both conditions (SM/TR) and compared to the same participants walk - ing without an exoskeleton (NE condition). The first part of this analysis consisted of the comparison of the speed difference between TR and SM conditions. The speed difference was obtained by computing, for each user, the difference between SM and TR median gait speeds, nor - malized by TR median speed. The second part compares NE to the TR and SM conditions during overground walking in terms of preferred gait speed, foot clearance (i.e., maximum height of the ankle joint during the swing phase), and stride length (i.e., distance between consecu - tive heel strikes of the same foot). For the NE, kinematic features were extracted using MediaPipe (Google) pose landmarks detection [ 28]. Another aspect of the analy - sis focuses on hip and knee angles for the gait cycle. The same benchmark dataset used to extract previously defined equilibrium angles [ 1] was used for this analy - sis. Notably, we present the average joint angles of users walking on a treadmill at a matching speed of 0.75 m/s (the same speed used to select exoskeleton parameters with the SM). The average joint angles from the bench - mark dataset for ramps and stairs use the same incline (5 deg) and step height (150 mm) as the exoskeleton experi - ment. Data were shown using a gait cycle defined from heel strike to heel strike, identified through marker-based methods for the benchmark dataset. To further assess the consistency of the phase detection framework across control conditions, we compared the percentage of gait cycle spent in each of the four gait states (Early Stance, Late Stance, Early Swing, Late Swing) between the trans - parent and state-machine controllers. We computed across all activities and participants, the average differ - ence across states (with the standard deviation reflecting inter-subject variability), to investigate if the gait phase detection was consistent across condition. Results In this section, we present the performance of the two controllers used during data collection, namely the State- Machine (SM) and the transparent (TR) conditions. We compared the two conditions by observing the amount of assistance provided, through interaction power, during different locomotion modes. Furthermore, we analyzed the kinematic differences in the locomotion patterns. Interaction power On average, the SM controller gave greater assistance during movement to users, compared to the TR control - ler. Interaction power represents the amount of assis - tance provided to the user during the execution of the movement. On this scale, negative values denote assis - tance, and more negative values indicate a higher magni - tude of assistance. Figure 4 shows the interaction power for different activities and different controllers. Combin - ing all activities, the SM controller has a lower interac - tion power than TR: SM: −2.6 × 10−2 ± 1.6 × 10−2 W/ kg, TR: 0.8 × 10−2 ± 0.7 × 10−2 W/kg). We found that in general, the interaction power was lower and statisti - cally different ( p-value < 0.005) for the state-machine controller, meaning more assistance compared to the transparent controller. Discussion All 19 participants successfully performed the five loco - motion activities (W, RA, RD, SA, SD) under both con - troller conditions. In this section, we will discuss the results presented in the previous section, highlighting the advantages and limitations of the SM and TR controllers, as well as potential future research directions enabled by the dataset collected and shared. Parameter identification for effective assistance in exoskeleton locomotion The extraction of parameters from a benchmark dataset provided equilibrium angles to enable effective assis - tance with the State Machine (SM) controller. However, Fig. 8 Difficulty Score (ranging between 1 and 5) measured questionaries completed after each condition. The plots display the median score across the experiment and 95% CI. Grey dots represent each user’s answer in the last iteration of each condition and activity. Only 13 of the 19 answered the questionnaire fully Page 13 of 18 Lhoste et al. Journal of NeuroEngineering and Rehabilitation (2026) 23:114 exoskeleton-assisted locomotion differs kinematically from unassisted walking (Figs. 6, 7) due to hardware constraints that limit movement, especially in the fron - tal and transverse planes. To compensate, we manually adjusted some parameters (Sect. State-machine control- ler) based on user feedback, primarily addressing sagittal plane movement. One limitation of parameter extraction is that we did not account for individual user preferences in walking patterns, relying instead on manual adjust - ments. We chose this approach to maintain consistency with the patient populations that we aim to address in the future. For these populations (spinal cord injury or stroke patients), this process would still require a calibration guided by a physical therapist, similar to [ 12] in pros - thetic SM approaches. Negative interaction power highlights the SM assistance As an extension of our previous work [ 11], the TR con - troller condition demonstrated the ability to minimize the absolute value of interaction power with the user in all locomotion modes. In most of the activities, this con - troller resulted in positive values of interaction power (i.e., resistive) but close to zero. The conditions in which the TR controller generated a high resistive interaction power with the user (greater than 0 W/kg) were at the hip joint during W, RA, and SA. Because this resistive power increased across these three activities, this sug - gests a relationship between interaction power and the amplitude of the hip extension. This effect may be due to increased friction as the joint approaches its limit or an underestimation of the gravitational influence of the leg On the other hand, SM controller demonstrated, in general, negative values of interaction power. The only exception was the knee joint during SA, in which high variance across users was measured with an average value close to zero. We think this behavior could be due to the different strategies the users choose to implement when climbing stairs. In fact, the hardware constraints (i.e., no lateral actuation) imposed by the exoskeleton require users to rely less on weight shifting. Another possibility is that the choice of a damping coefficient was too low in this locomotion activity, creating a viscous behavior dur - ing the interaction. Another interesting observation is the behavior of the knee joint during stair descent (SD). We believe the low interaction power observed was pri - marily due to how users performed the stair descent. The X2 exoskeleton’s limited ankle range of motion necessi - tates increased knee flexion during SD to reach the next step. This behavior is not present in the benchmark data- set, further motivating the need to collect and appropri - ately address this limitation in future assistive controllers. State-machine controller increases speed, kinematics match, perceived ease of walking Gait speed analysis suggests that the SM controller enables users to perform various locomotion activities at faster speeds, with the exception of stair descent (Fig. 5). In SD, the assistance was particularly high (indicated by very negative interaction power) for the knee joint. In addition, since most participants lacked prior experi - ence with the exoskeleton, they may have intentionally reduced their speed to prioritize safety, particularly on stairs descent. A longer familiarization phase or addi - tional training sessions could influence self-selected speeds for each locomotion activity. At the same time, results displayed in Fig. 6 showed that both controllers result in slower preferred gait speeds compared to walking without an exoskeleton. Similar results apply to foot clearance and stride length, though the SM condition results in closer spatial gait compared to the users’ natural patterns. A limitation of this analysis is how these values were calculated. Mediapipe was used in the NE condition, while the joint encoders were used in the TR and SM conditions. In the future, a more uni - form system will be used to compare the locomotion fea - tures across all three conditions. The same considerations apply to Fig. 7. The kinemat- ics profiles were extracted using a different set of sensors and users, and walking speed could be different in subset of activities (stairs, ramps). Nevertheless, some consid - erations are still possible. First, during all activities, the hip extension of the SM trajectory matches the bench - mark dataset better than the TR trajectory. This result is ambiguous and potentially arising from either of two underlying mechanisms. In fact, it might suggest a more natural walking pattern (1). At the same time, the amount of positive interaction power (meaning resistance) mea - sured at the hip joint during that specific locomotion suggests that kinematics extracted from users walking without an exoskeleton may not scale adequately to exo - skeleton locomotion (2). The analysis of the participant questionnaires supports hypothesis (1) suggesting a more natural walking pattern. In fact, users reported that was easier to perform the five activities in the SM condition compared to the TR condition (Fig. 8). A second consideration regarding Sect. Kinematics and questionnaires and Fig. 7 is that the SM and TR condi - tions exhibit similar kinematic profiles. This indicates that users performed comparable movements regardless of whether explicit assistance was provided by the exo - skeleton. In particular, a delay in the swing was observed across all activities. This is in line with previous studies [29]. An explanation of this behavior could be the need to move their center of mass (and the one of the exoskel - eton) on the stance leg before transitioning to the swing, delaying the activity of the controller. An additional Page 14 of 18 Lhoste et al. Journal of NeuroEngineering and Rehabilitation (2026) 23:114 consideration is that participants used crutches for level ground walking and parallel bars in other activities to safely perform all activities, resulting in possible differ - ences in their gait patterns. Future research will focus on reducing this delay by leveraging machine learning tech - niques that utilize kinematic data instead of force-sensing resistor sensors to proactively predict the transition from stance to swing [ 10]. Finally, the differences in SD trajec - tories between the SM and TR conditions compared to the NE condition can be attributed to the limited range of motion of the X2 exoskeleton, particularly at the ankle. This limitation required participants to adopt a taught strategy for descending stairs: exaggerating hip flexion to ensure the middle of the foot landed on the edge of each step. Unimpaired gait data with exoskeleton provides valuable insights This study provides an additional benchmark data- set that could promote collaboration and accelerate the translation of research findings into clinical and real- world applications. For example, this dataset could allow other research groups to replicate the parameter extrac - tion presented in this work with an exoskeleton-specific benchmark dataset. In addition, it also presents novel information on how unimpaired individuals walk using a lower-limb exoskeleton with and without explicitly defined assistance. In particular, the recorded interaction forces between the user and the exoskeleton can provide insight into how individuals respond to the exoskeleton trajectory with the purpose of providing an assistive con - troller that optimizes the interaction power. This infor - mation could be used to define new locomotion policies that minimize the cost functions analyzed in this work: interaction power, gait speed, and subjective evaluation. Using human-in-the-loop approaches, researchers can assess how users respond to specific robot policies and adapt them accordingly. We believe that our dataset provides a valuable resource for training and validating machine learning algorithms for exoskeleton control, for each level of the hierarchical control (e.g., high-level to recognize the activity, mid-level to recognize the gait state [ 10], etc.) or in fusing the different levels in a single controller [ 23]. Indeed, we believe that machine learning algorithms can be designed to generate more effective assistance profiles for various types of locomotion while also generalizing to new activities (e.g., obstacle avoidance, sit-to-stand) and new users. This approach could significantly reduce cali - bration time and mark a step toward more intuitive and user-friendly lower-limb exoskeletons. Conclusion First, it introduces a state-machine-based control strat - egy for partial-assistance lower-limb exoskeletons. Sec - ond, it presents a computational method for extracting reference trajectories from a benchmark dataset, facilitat- ing the identification of state-machine controller param - eters and streamlining calibration. Third, it provides a dataset of 19 healthy individuals walking under five conditions (overground, upstairs, downstairs, up ramps, and down ramps) using either a state-machine approach or a transparent controller. This dataset provides a valu - able resource for the research community to explore and advance the field. Evaluation of our state machine showed increased preferred walking speed with respect to transparent control, closer kinematics to the bench - mark dataset, and a reduction in perceived effort. While the proposed control strategy demonstrates potential, it is not without limitations (e.g., optimality of chosen con - trol parameters, limited user training, restricted num - ber of degrees-of-freedom of hardware). Future research should aim to overcome these limitations by utilizing the shared dataset to develop more robust and effective con - trol mechanisms, with validation on patient populations such as individuals with neurological conditions such as incomplete spinal cord injury and stroke. The vectors Am ∈ R4 and Bm ∈ R4 are used in Eq. 9 and represent the coefficients for each gait phase (in order E-St, L-St, E-Sw, L-Sw). Some considerations are possible regarding these parameters. The parameters Am represent the slope of the linear regressor, multiplying the task condition (c) for the four phases of locomotion (E-St, L-St, E-Sw, L-Sw). A large magnitude indicates a strong relationship between the task condition and the desired equilibrium angle. In particular, regarding overground walking, it can be observed that the hip equilibrium angles during early stance and early swing are mostly unaffected (− 0.02 and − 0.03 respectively) by the choice of gait speed. In con - trast, for the other two gait phases (L-St and L-Sw), the equilibrium angles are highly influenced by gait speed. In particular, for late stance, the fit exhibits a strong negative slope (− 0.19) due to the larger range of motion in the hip extension phase of the gait cycle while late swing exhib - its a positive slope (0.13) that amplifies hip flexion when the speed increases. On the other hand, the knee param - eters exhibit a complementary behavior. Specifically, E-Sw shows a steeper slope (− 0.14), reflecting greater knee flex- ion as velocity increases. During ramp ascent, the hip angles are highly influ - enced by the slope inclination, particularly during the swing phase (E-Sw and L-Sw, both 2.29). This is due to greater hip flexion as the ramp slope increases. This also explains the behavior of the knee angle param- eters, resulting in highly negative Am values in L-Sw and E-St (− 2.29 and − 1.72, respectively). In these phases, the knee must first prepare for ground contact at a higher level relative to the other foot and then sustain that posi - tion during the transition to L-St, which remains mostly unaffected (-0.57). As for ramp descent, it can be observed that in the early stance, early swing, and late swing phases (0.57, 0.57, and 0.0, respectively), the ramp incline has little or no effect on the hip joint equilibrium angles. However, in the late stance phase (1.71), an increase in the equilibrium angle is observed as the inclination angle increases. Conversely, the knee equilibrium angles are influenced by the ramp slope, exhibiting greater knee flexion across all phases of ramp descent as the slope increases. This results in the exoskeleton adopting a more crouched posture. Finally, for stairs, all phases in both cases (ascent and descent) show strong trends. Stair ascent exhibits a strong positive trend for the hip angle during swing (2.1 and 2.5 in E-Sw and L-Sw, respectively) due to the need for greater hip flexion while reaching the next step. Stair descent demonstrates an additional strong relationship in L-Sw (2.4), as adopting a crouched posture is necessary during the execution of the steps. Knee angles are also influenced by step size. During stair ascent, the parameters Am pres- ent a negative slope in E-St (− 2.5), E-Sw (− 2.8), and L-Sw (− 3.9), indicating the need for additional knee flexion in preparation for contact with the next step. Once the step is reached, in L-St, the leg is almost fully extended to bring the body over the step, resulting in no relationship observed in this phase. Conversely, during stair descent, the robot assumes a crouched posture to reach the lower step while supporting the user’s weight in that position. The lower the step, the more the knee is flexed. Conse - quently, L-St in stair descent is the phase most influenced by step height (− 4.6).
