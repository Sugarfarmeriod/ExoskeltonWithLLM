Vol.:(0123456789) Journal of Intelligent & Robotic Systems (2025) 111:24 https://doi.org/10.1007/s10846-025-02230-7 REGULAR PAPER A Lower Limb Exoskeleton Adaptive Control Method Based on Model‑free Reinforcement Learning and Improved Dynamic Movement Primitives Liping Huang1 · Jianbin Zheng2 · Yifan Gao2 · Qiuzhi Song3 · Yali Liu3 Received: 4 January 2023 / Accepted: 15 January 2025 / Published online: 11 February 2025 © The Author(s) 2025 Abstract Recent advancements in lower limb exoskeleton control have predominantly focused on enhancing walking capabilities across diverse terrains, such as level ground, stairs, and ramps. However, achieving seamless transitions between these terrains remains a significant challenge due to the unpredictability of the environment, which hampers adaptive control. In this paper, we propose a Hierarchical Interactive Learning (HIL) control method based on gait phase and locomotion pattern recognition. The method comprises two layers: high-level learning and low-level control. The high-level learning is based on gait phase and locomotion pattern recognition, utilizing the Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMP) to piecewise learn the desired joint torque curves. The low-level control utilizes the learned DMP to output torque based on the gait phase and locomotion pattern, while reinforcement learning is employed to dynamically adjust the control parameters of DMP in real-time with the goal of minimizing human-exoskeleton interaction forces. The experiments collected gait data of lower limb movement from active exoskeletons. The results show that our method significantly reduces human-exoskeleton interaction forces across diverse terrains. In order to verify the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed method, 15 healthy subjects were tested with the lower limb exoskeleton of these 3 generations. The experimental results show that the proposed HIL control method gives a valuable tool for smooth transitions among different terrains, reduces the reliance on accurate dynamic models and the average oxygen consumption decreased by about 12%, underscoring its potential to improve exoskeleton-assisted mobility. Keywords Dynamic movement primitives · Reinforcement learning · Human-exoskeleton interaction · Hierarchical interactive control · Lower limb exoskeleton 1 Introduction In addition to ensuring safety, precision, and robustness, exoskeleton robots need to meet the key requirement of reducing human-exoskeleton interaction force in different terrains to enhance human movement comfort and reduce oxygen consumption [1]. In human-exoskeleton interaction applications, comfort means that the human-exoskeleton coupling system must resemble natural human behavior. To achieve this goal, the exoskeleton controller should be able to real-time follow the human's intended movements and minimize the interaction forces between the human and the exoskeleton as much as possible. Considering the wearable comfort and motion stability requirements of the human- exoskeleton system, the exoskeleton needs to have rapid response and favorable tracking performance in order to reduce human-exoskeleton impedance. The model-based controllers, such as impedance modulation control, are rela- tively simple but cannot meet the requirements due to dif- ficulties in scheduling gain. Meanwhile, accurately defining model-based control methods poses a challenge, given the intricate dynamics and nonlinearities inherent in the interac- tion between human and exoskeleton [2, 3]. The control of an exoskeleton should align with the wearer's intentions to offer effective support and achieve * Jianbin Zheng jbzheng@whut.edu.cn 1 School of Computer and Information Engineering, Henan University of Economics and Law, Zhengzhou 450046, China 2 School of Information Engineering, Wuhan University of Technology, Wuhan 430070, China 3 School of Mechatronical Engineering, Beijing Institute of Technology, Beijing 100081, China Journal of Intelligent & Robotic Systems (2025) 111:24 24 Page 2 of 20 coordinated movement objectives. Gait phase detection and locomotion pattern recognition serve as valuable indicators of human intent for controlling exoskeletons, motivating researchers to actualize the functionality of active exoskel- etons in load-bearing walking scenarios. In load-bearing walking scenarios, how to plan assistive torque curve to make the exoskeleton simulate human natu- ral locomotion is the focus of research [4 –7]. To the best of our knowledge, there are two major challenges of using torque planning for the exoskeleton [8]: (1) Insufficient sta- bility: The control effect of the exoskeleton is determined by the reliability of the sensors, the accuracy of locomotion patterns recognition, and the executive ability of the motor controllers. Any errors in these cascade control systems will result in movement deviation against the expected movement of the humans; (2) Insufficient comfort: Due to the disparity between the rigid mechanical exoskeleton structure and the pliable human body, existing exoskeleton systems face chal- lenges in providing optimal comfort during wear. Unlike the human musculoskeletal system, which relies on the direct influence of soft muscles attached to bones, exoskeleton systems are externally supported and powered by mechani- cal components. Consequently, when humans engage with exoskeletons, impediments arise in the form of barriers like skin and muscle tissue, situated between the exoskeleton and the human skeletal system. During interaction, the exoskel- eton squeezes on the body's soft tissues, making the human uncomfortable. In addition, compressed soft tissue also con- sumes energy, leading to an increase in metabolic cost. At present, the state-of-the-art methods have some solu- tions in response to these two challenges. The predefined torque control strategy is to define the assistive torque pro- vided during the movement of the exoskeleton in advance, and invoke the corresponding assistive torque according to the current gait phase, so as to achieve the assisted walking of the human. Keehong Seo et al. applied the control strategy of predefined torque to the hip joint assist for synchronous and adaptive human–robot interaction [9]. Considering that the motion dynamics of human individuals cannot be accu- rately modeled by mathematical description, He Huang et al. proposed a tracking control scheme based on Reinforcement Learning (RL) to completely simulate the locomotion state of exoskeleton lower extremity knee joint under different tasks[10, 11]. Gait trajectory prediction is an important method to adapt to the gait variations of exoskeleton wear - ers. This approach defines a trajectory model and modifies the reference trajectory based on the minimization of inter - action forces under boundary constraints [12–16]. Although the reference trajectory can to some extent adapt to motion variations, the adaptability to significant changes commonly encountered in locomotion pattern variations is restricted by the optimization constraints and motion pattern-specific model limitations. Essentially, the underlying cause of these challenges is that physiological and neurological differences between indi- viduals can cause the same controller to react differently in different individuals, meaning that an optimal control strat- egy for one subject may not work well for another. This requires that the goal of human-in-the-loop optimization is no longer to build a “perfect” algorithm, but to constantly judge and interact with the exoskeleton worn during the human movement [17–20]. Inspired by this, the idea of this paper is to separate human intention recognition and real- time control into high and low levels. The high level is the learning process of joint desired torques for different human intentions recognized, while the low-level control uses model-free reinforcement learning to update the parameters of the DMP model online to minimize human-exoskeleton interaction forces, better achieving load-assisted control in complex environments. In addition, the wearer and the exoskeleton are bound with flexible fabric, and the motion trajectories of the exo- skeleton and the wearer are not completely aligned. When the exoskeleton provides assistance, there is an interaction force between the wearer and the exoskeleton. In order to maintain natural movement, the wearer will sometimes force actively the exoskeleton to move to complete the intended gait (resulting in increased oxygen consumption). There- fore, the interaction force is adopted to describe the per - formance of the control strategy in this paper. In the pro- cess of movement, the smaller the interaction force is, the smaller the exoskeleton’s hindrance to human is, and the better the movement compliance is. Minimizing interaction force becomes the goal of the control strategy, meanwhile, it can lower metabolism and reduce the interference of the exoskeleton against human movement. Therefore, this paper proposes a Hierarchical Interac- tive Learning (HIL) control method, which is built on the foundation of human intention recognition, and sequentially learns the wearer's joint expected torque curve and real-time control parameters of the human-exoskeleton interaction model. This method consists of two levels: high-level learn- ing and low-level control. The input of high-level learning is the result of motion intention recognition. Joint expected tor- ques are learned offline based on different motion intentions, and the wearer's motion curves are segmented and modeled using DMP. A locally weighted regression algorithm [21, 22] is used to offline learn the DMP model parameters under different motion intentions, with the output being the opti- mal weights W∗ DMP of each Gaussian kernel fitting function. The input of low-level control consists of two parts: one part is the result of motion intentions recognition, calling the cor- responding DMP model for different motion intentions, and the other part is the input of the angle difference feedback between the exoskeleton and the wearer. The parameters of the DMP model are updated online using reinforcement Journal of Intelligent & Robotic Systems (2025) 111:24 Page 3 of 20 24 learning to adapt to the dynamic interaction of different ter- rains according to the angle difference feedback, and adjust output torque in real-time. The DMP is a dynamic modeling method based on non- linear systems proposed by Ijspeert et al. [23–26]. The basic idea of the DMP is to adopt the second-order dynamic sys- tem that meets Lyapunov stability to construct an “attractive cycle” model [27, 28]. By learning the motion information of target trajectory, the newly generated trajectory has the motion trend of cycle trajectory, such that the motion state of the system can be controlled by changing the “attractive cycle” model parameters. The HIL online control method proposed in this paper is similar to the method proposed by Huang et al. [29]. The paper of Huang et al. adopts the DMP to model and learn the human's locomotion curve, which requires a complete gait cycle to re-learn the new DMP models. Then when the locomotion pattern changes frequently, the DMP cannot learn and update the control parameter in real time, which leads to the increase of the interaction force. In order to solve this problem, this paper improves the DMP model by add- ing coupling parameters, and adopts reinforcement learning method to dynamically adjust the coupling parameter values, so that the controller can update in real time according to the human intention. In the low-level con- trol, the control strategy calls the corresponding DMP model according to the gait phase and locomotion pattern obtained by the human intention recognition module, and updates the parameter factor Θ via reinforcement learning to achieve the optimal output. 1.2 Joint Torque Curve Learning Offline Based on DMP and Coupling Item Updating Based on RL Online Based on the recognition of human locomotion intention, the piecewise off-line modeling of joint expected torque is car- ried out by DMP, and the optimal weight coefficient W∗ DMP of the forced term (i.e. nonlinear function f) of DMP model is obtained by non-parametric local weighted regression learning method. In order to optimize the control of terrain adaptability, the admittance model between the wearer and the lower limb exoskeleton based on HRI force is adopted, and the model is introduced into the DMP model by adding coupling terms. A model-free reinforcement learning method based on PI2 algorithm is employed to learn the coupling item during online control phase. The PI2 algorithm can transform the differential equation into a path integral problem, so that the value function can be obtained by solving the path inte- gral. Since the coupling item represents the control deviation and system noise, the negative value of the coupling item is taken as the action of reinforcement learning, which is added into the DMP model to eliminate the influence of the coupling item on the system as much as possible. The return of reinforcement learning is the HRI force. Since we want the agent of RL to take as few steps as possible to reach the desired torque, the real-time cost of each gait cycle is set as negative value. The higher the cumulative return is, the less the exoskeleton and human angle difference is, the less the HRI force is, the better the control of the system will be. 1.2.1 Obtaining the Optimal Weight Coefficient W∗ DMP Based on DMP The DMP is a dynamic modeling method proposed by Ijspeert et al. based on nonlinear systems. The simulation protocols are as follows: (1) The proposed method is tested via the Open source Gazebo 7.0 simulation software; (2)The input of the iDMP model are four gait phases (i.e. IOn, On, IOff, and Off) and two locomotion patterns (level ground W and stair descent SD), and the output of the iDMP model is the torque T(φ); (3)The interaction torque is the difference between the expected torque and the model output torque. The Ks and Kd in Eq. 11 are set to 0.1 kg∙m 2/s2 and 0.2 kg∙m 2/s, Ck in Eq. 12 is set to 0, both the weight coefficients β1 and β2 in Eq. 22 are set to 500, and the force arm is set to 0.3 m. The proposed iDMP model is applied to a hierarchical interaction framework, and the simulation control process is shown in Fig. 5. The input of the exoskeleton control system is divided into two parts: the human intention and the angle difference between the exoskeleton mechanical thigh and the human thigh. The former is used as the basis of the Gaussian kernel function called by DMP to output the torque T(φ), and the latter is used as the input of RL. The output of the system is the sum of T(φ) and At. The control system calls the cor- responding Gaussian kernel function set according to the recognized human intention, obtains the output torque T(φ), and makes the difference between it and the expected torque, and the result is regarded as the penalty term of RL. The difference that is σT = ED(φ )- T(φ ) is taken as the penalty term of reinforcement learning. The RL takes the human- exoskeleton thigh angle difference as input and minimizes the penalty term (i.e., HRI force) by taking actions At, and adds it with the nonlinear function f to achieve the goal of adjusting the output torque T(φ) and minimizing the penalty term. We adopted similar HIL method [45] for comparing the proposed iDMP method. In [45], the DMP is also adopted to model and learn the locomotion trajectory. Prior to Fig. 5 Flowchart of offline negative feedback simulation of iDMP model based on RL Journal of Intelligent & Robotic Systems (2025) 111:24 24 Page 10 of 20 controlling the exoskeleton by iDMP, Gaussian weights are trained by sampled human locomotion trajectories. We set the Gaussian number in iDMP to 24 and employed LWR method to learn Gaussian weights incrementally. After acquiring the weight coefficients of iDMP, the exoskeleton will learn the optimal parameter Θ for several gait cycles according to the introduction in the part of Section 3.4, so that the tracking cost CTR converges. In this paper, the exoskeleton updates the parameters every four gait cycles (k = 4) and calculate the tracking cost CTR every one gait cycle. We conduct two experiments to verify the effectiveness of the proposed iDMP model. The first experiment evalu- ated the efficiency of RL. Due to the limitation of the applica- tion of exoskeleton-embedded environment and the require- ment of real-time control, many advanced control algorithms have not been adopted, and the current work is mainly focused on regular terrain conditions (such as SA, RA, SD and RD) assistance. In the future, we will apply the proposed method to the assisted exoskeleton under irregular terrain conditions, and more fully verify the assisted exoskeleton to the adaptation performance of more complex environment. The degree of decrease in oxygen consumption depends on various factors such as the mechanical structure, actua- tion joints, motion patterns, and control methods of the exo- skeleton worn by the user. These have been compared with exoskeleton control methods similar to international assisted mechanisms, as shown in Table 3. Compared with recent literature, the results of oxygen consumption reduction in this study have reached inter - national standards, with a decrease of approximately 12% in the user's oxygen consumption. However, since the subjects regularly participated in the exoskeleton adjust- ment and were familiar with the exoskeleton's control characteristics, there is a certain degree of subjectivity or uncertainty in the results of oxygen consumption reduc- tion. Nevertheless, the reduction in oxygen consumption is still essential for the user, as it can prolong endurance during physical activity, reduce fatigue, and significantly improve energy efficiency during high-intensity exercise. 1.4 Conclusion and Future Works The paper has proposed an HIL control method for the adap- tive control of a human-centered wearable exoskeleton. The iDMP model can offline learn the human’s joint expected torque. The RL based on PI2 algorithm is adopted to update online the parameters of the iDMP model, which adapts to changing of the HRI force. We evaluated the iDMP model performance on different exoskeleton platforms. High-level learning is the learning process of joint expected torque about different locomotion pattern. Low-level control by model-free reinforcement learning for exoskeleton control- ler parameter Θ of the iDMP model is to adapt to complex assist environment. The experimental results show that our proposed HIL control method can better handle the different interaction dynamics on different locomotion patterns. In addition, in the subsequent high-level learning process, it is planned to adopt a more flexible modeling approach and try some model-based control methods (such as the central pattern generator model [48, 49]). These methods can generate rhythm pattern output data without feedback from sensor data, further improving control robustness in complex terrains.
